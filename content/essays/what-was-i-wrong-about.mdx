What Was I Wrong About?
One of the advantages of getting older is that reality eventually corrects many of your assumptions.
One of the disadvantages is realizing how many assumptions needed correcting.
If there is a common theme across my personal and professional journey, it is this:
I have rarely suffered because I lacked information.
I have often suffered because I misunderstood how the world actually works.
That realization has made me increasingly interested in identifying things I was wrong about.
Not because being wrong is enjoyable.
Because it is educational.
The lessons that change us most are often the ones that challenge our certainty.
Looking back, several stand out.
1. I Thought Hard Work Was Enough
Growing up, I believed effort explained most outcomes.
Work harder.
Stay disciplined.
Keep improving.
Success would eventually follow.
There is truth in that belief.
Hard work matters.
A great deal.
But reality is more nuanced.
Hard work without direction creates exhaustion.
1

Hard work without opportunity creates frustration.
Hard work without leverage creates limitations.
The world rewards effort.
It also rewards positioning.
Timing.
Networks.
Access.
Systems.
Understanding this did not make me value hard work less.
It made me appreciate everything else that matters alongside it.
2. I Underestimated The Importance Of Opportunity
For years, I focused heavily on talent.
Finding talent.
Developing talent.
Recognizing talent.
Over time, I encountered too many examples to ignore.
Brilliant people trapped by circumstance.
Average people transformed by access.
Capable individuals overlooked because nobody knew they existed.
The pattern became impossible to miss.
Talent is widespread.
Opportunity is not.
2

That realization fundamentally changed how I think about hiring, development, entrepreneurship, and
social mobility.
Today, if someone asks me what changes lives most consistently, my answer is simple:
Opportunity.
3. I Thought Knowledge Was The Ultimate Advantage
At one point, I believed the people with the most knowledge would inevitably win.
The internet challenged that assumption.
Then AI challenged it even further.
Knowledge is becoming increasingly accessible.
The advantage is shifting.
From possessing information.
To interpreting information.
From memorizing answers.
To asking better questions.
From expertise alone.
To judgment.
This may be one of the biggest changes in my thinking over the last decade.
4. I Underestimated Systems
Earlier in life, I focused heavily on individuals.
Exceptional people fascinated me.
Founders.
Leaders.
3

Builders.
Visionaries.
I still admire exceptional people.
But I increasingly study systems.
Because systems explain why outcomes repeat.
A great individual can achieve extraordinary things.
A great system can make extraordinary outcomes more common.
The larger the challenge, the more important systems become.
That lesson continues influencing everything I build.
5. I Thought Success Would Feel Different
This one surprised me.
Like many people, I assumed success would feel transformative.
That certain milestones would permanently change how life felt.
The reality was different.
The milestones mattered.
The satisfaction was real.
But it was temporary.
The mind quickly adapted.
New goals emerged.
New challenges appeared.
The journey continued.
This taught me something important.
4

Happiness cannot depend entirely on future achievements.
Because the future keeps moving.
Contentment must exist alongside ambition.
Not after it.
6. I Misunderstood Power
For a long time, power felt distant.
Almost irrelevant.
Something that existed in institutions, politics, and executive circles.
Then I encountered it more directly.
Influence.
Networks.
Access.
Decision-making.
What changed my mind was not discovering that power exists.
Everyone knows that.
It was understanding how often it shapes outcomes.
The lesson was not to chase power.
The lesson was to understand it.
Because ignoring power creates blind spots.
Worshipping power creates distortions.
Understanding power creates awareness.
5

7. I Thought Geography Was Destiny
Growing up, certain places appeared to possess all the opportunities.
The major cities.
The global hubs.
The economic centers.
Everything important seemed to happen there.
Technology challenged that belief.
The internet reduced distance.
Remote work expanded access.
Digital infrastructure created new possibilities.
Geography still matters.
But it matters less than it once did.
That realization changed how I think about Odisha.
About India.
About opportunity.
About the future.
8. I Underestimated Time
Perhaps this is the biggest lesson of all.
I consistently underestimated compounding.
The power of staying committed.
The power of relationships maintained over years.
The power of skills practiced consistently.
6

The power of trust accumulated slowly.
The power of reputation built patiently.
Most meaningful achievements happen gradually.
Then suddenly.
The gradual part is where most people leave.
The sudden part is where outsiders begin paying attention.
Time rewards consistency more than intensity.
That lesson continues revealing itself.
Looking Back
If I am fortunate, this list will continue growing.
Not because I enjoy being wrong.
Because I enjoy learning.
The people who stop updating their beliefs eventually stop updating themselves.
Reality changes.
Technology changes.
Societies change.
Opportunities change.
Our understanding should evolve as well.
The goal is not to become a person who is always right.
The goal is to become a person who is willing to become less wrong.
That may sound like a small distinction.
It is not.
7

Because progress often begins where certainty ends.
And some of the most valuable lessons in life arrive disguised as mistakes.
Learning Outcome
Growth is not about proving yourself right. It is about becoming less wrong over time. The willingness to
update your beliefs in response to reality is one of the strongest indicators of intellectual maturity.
