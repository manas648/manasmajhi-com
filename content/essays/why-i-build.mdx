Three weeks into one of our early searches, the pipeline had silently stopped. No obvious failure — the outreach was correct, the role was real, the messaging was right. But responses had stopped completely. It took reconstructing the work week by week to find where it had broken. What eventually worked was video messages. Three candidates came back. The placement succeeded. I replayed that sequence for days afterward — not because it had worked, but because of what finding the break had felt like.

That is why I build.

## The origin of compulsion

Three weeks in, we were behind. The search was for an Associate Partner at a management consulting firm — niche domain expertise, a limited talent pool, and a commitment we had made to the client: one qualified candidate submitted every week.

We had not delivered in three weeks.

Going back through the work, nothing was obviously wrong. The outreach was going to the right people. The messaging was correct. The role was real. But responses had stopped — LinkedIn, email, phone calls that went to voicemail. No signal. The problem only became visible when we reconstructed what had happened manually, week by week, until we found the moment the pipeline had silently stopped working.

What eventually worked was video messages — short, specific, direct. Three candidates came back. The placement succeeded.

But the three weeks of pressure, the manual archaeology, the improvised pivot — none of that was inevitable. It was the cost of operating without the infrastructure that would have shown us the pattern forming before it became a crisis.

That is what I kept running into. The tools existed for sourcing. The tools existed for tracking. Nothing existed for the thing that actually mattered: knowing whether a search was working, why it was failing, and what to do about it before it collapsed.

So I started building. Not because I planned to build a software company. Because the alternative — continuing to operate without the infrastructure I knew should exist — was intolerable.

## What building actually costs

Building is expensive in ways that are hard to communicate to people who haven't done it.

It costs time in a way that is qualitatively different from working for someone else. When you work for someone else, you can leave the problem at the end of the day. When you are building, the problem follows you everywhere.

It costs certainty. Those three weeks in the consulting search — doing everything correctly, getting no signal, not knowing where the problem was — that was not an anomaly. That is the permanent condition of building. You make decisions based on incomplete information and trust that the direction is right even when the destination is unclear. The difference is that the stakes are yours.

It costs ego. The market does not care about your vision. It cares about whether what you built solves a real problem at a price people will pay. Learning this — really learning it, not just knowing it — is a process of sustained humbling.

## What building returns

But here is what it returns: the experience of making something that did not exist before.

Not metaphorically. Literally. The system I am building — [Majhi OS](/hiring/the-future-belongs-to-operational-intelligence) — did not exist before I started building it. The searches it recovers would have failed. The hiring velocity it creates would not have existed. The insight it generates would have been lost.

That is not nothing.

Building also returns compounding. Every problem you solve makes you better at solving the next one. Every system you build teaches you what you didn't know about systems. The learning is not linear. I understand more about how organizations fail and recover from running Majhi Group searches for five years than I could have understood any other way. I understand more about what recruiting teams actually need from watching what they do when the tools they have are inadequate. The understanding is operational — it comes from being in the problem, not studying it from the outside.

## What building Majhi Group taught me about trust

Running an executive search firm is, at its core, a trust business. The client trusts you with one of the most consequential decisions their company will make. The candidate trusts you with their career at a moment when they are considering a significant change. Both sides are making themselves vulnerable in specific ways.

What this taught me is that trust is not built through competence alone. Competence is the baseline — you have to be good at the work. But trust is built through transparency about what you know and don't know, through honesty about when something is going wrong, and through the consistency of following through on small commitments that didn't have to be honored.

I have learned this by getting it wrong sometimes. The client I should have told earlier that the brief was miscalibrated. The candidate I should have been more direct with about the timeline. The situations where I was managing people's expectations rather than telling them the truth. Every one of those was a lesson in what trust actually requires.

Building Majhi Group has been an education in relationships at the level of real stakes. Not the managed relationships of a corporate environment, but the direct accountability of a business where every outcome is traceable back to a decision I made.

## The deeper answer

But the deepest answer is this: I build because I believe that the gap between how things are and how things should be is closeable.

Not through wishing. Through work. Through building the systems and tools and processes that make the better version of things possible.

I grew up in [Kalahandi](/kalahandi/growing-up-in-kalahandi) — a district with genuine resources and genuine capability that has never had the institutional infrastructure to convert those inputs into the outcomes they could produce. That gap — between what exists and what is possible — was visible to me before I had language for it. And the question it left me with, which I have never fully put down, is whether the gap is closeable.

The answer, from everything I have seen, is yes. Not easily. Not quickly. But yes — through the patient work of building the systems that translate what exists into what's possible.

The world has too many people who see the gap and despair. And not enough people who see the gap and start building.

I would rather be the second kind.
