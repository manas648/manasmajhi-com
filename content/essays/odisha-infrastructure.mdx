When I was setting up our first office in Kalahandi — a ten-seater delivery center in one of India's most historically underconnected districts — the infrastructure question was not abstract. It was the first conversation every prospective client wanted to have. Power. Internet. Roads. Could people actually get there?

The honest answer then was: barely. The honest answer now is more complicated, and more interesting. Odisha's infrastructure has shifted in the last decade from a reliable liability to an uneven asset — strong in some places, still genuinely limited in others, and improving at a pace that most external observers haven't tracked.

## Ports: the infrastructure story that matters most

The single biggest change in Odisha's physical infrastructure position is ports. Paradip Port, on the northern coast, handles among the highest bulk cargo volumes of any Indian major port — iron ore exports, coal imports, fertiliser materials. Dhamra Port, with one of the deepest draughts on the east coast, now handles very large vessels for dry bulk and liquid cargo. Gopalpur adds southern coast capacity.

This three-port cluster is not a marginal improvement. Eastern India has historically been constrained by weak maritime trade infrastructure relative to the western coast. Gujarat and Maharashtra captured industrial investment partly because they were easier to ship from. As Sagarmala builds coastal industrial zones along Odisha's shore, that equation is changing. Companies that locate manufacturing in Odisha now have genuine port access — not just on paper, but operationally.

## Roads: meaningful progress, uneven delivery

The national highway network connecting Odisha has improved significantly. NH-16, running Kolkata to Chennai through Bhubaneswar, is a functioning trade artery for eastern India. The Bhubaneswar-Cuttack corridor is well connected. Mineral belt connectivity — the roads running from the iron ore and coal districts of Keonjhar, Sundargarh, and Angul to rail yards and ports — has improved, though geography keeps it expensive to maintain.

What remains genuinely difficult is the last mile. Odisha's interior geography — forested hills, river deltas, flood-prone coastal plains — makes rural road connectivity an ongoing challenge rather than a solved problem. Connecting agricultural markets to district headquarters, and district headquarters to state highways, is work that is progressing under national programs but is far from complete. I have driven enough of these roads to know the difference between what the maps show and what you actually navigate.

## Rail and power: solid where it matters for industry

Rail is the backbone of Odisha's industrial economy. The East Coast Railway zone, headquartered in Bhubaneswar, manages the network that moves iron ore and coal from extraction to port — the circulatory system of a mineral-driven economy. For industrial operations, rail connectivity is functional. For passenger travel between secondary cities, it is variable.

Power has been one of Odisha's genuine improvement stories. From chronic outages that were a standing joke in the 1990s, urban and industrial power has moved to something approaching reliability. OPGC generation, Vedanta's Jharsuguda capacity, and central sector allocations have changed the supply equation. Industrial operations can plan around their power needs in a way they could not fifteen years ago.

## Digital infrastructure: Bhubaneswar works; beyond that, it depends

Bhubaneswar's Smart Cities investment has produced real digital infrastructure — fibre connectivity, improved broadband, functional digital civic services. Internet quality in Bhubaneswar and Cuttack is competitive with any Tier 2 Indian city. For a technology services operation, the connectivity is adequate.

BharatNet has extended fibre to gram panchayat level across a significant portion of rural Odisha. The gap between what is installed and what is actually delivering reliable service remains large in many areas. Rural digital connectivity is better on paper than it is in practice — which is a fair description of BharatNet's national execution broadly, not specific to Odisha.

## The honest infrastructure question for someone building here

The question I had to answer when setting up in Kalahandi — can we actually operate here? — has a better answer now than it did then. For urban Bhubaneswar operations: yes, straightforwardly. For industrial operations in the mineral belt: yes, with adequate planning. For remote district operations: yes, but with more redundancy and more patience than the same operation would require in a tier-1 city.

Odisha's infrastructure is no longer the first reason to rule the state out. It is now a set of specific tradeoffs to understand rather than a blanket constraint. Not a completion — but a meaningful shift.
