Most conversations about opportunity treat it as binary. Either you have it or you don't. The intervention becomes: create more opportunity. Make the door available.

This misses the actual problem. The door being available is one condition among five. A person standing in front of a door they cannot open, in a building they cannot enter, for an opportunity they don't know exists — has access to zero of the opportunity that was theoretically available. Creating more doors does not solve the problem.

>> The door being available is one condition among five. Creating more doors does not solve the problem. Locate where the chain breaks — and fix the link that's broken.

The Opportunity Framework maps the full chain. Five links. Each enables the next. Each can fail independently. And when any one fails, the chain breaks — regardless of how well the other four are functioning.

## The chain

```
TALENT
  ↓
  [Does the person have the raw capability?]

ACCESS
  ↓
  [Can they reach the opportunity? Do they have
   the information, networks, and systems?]

OPPORTUNITY
  ↓
  [Does the door open? Is the moment real?]

MOBILITY
  ↓
  [Can they convert the opportunity into
   actual movement — economically, socially,
   geographically?]

PROSPERITY
  [Does the movement compound?
   Does it change what the next
   generation starts with?]
```

## Stage 1: Talent

Talent is the raw material. It is, as far as any serious analysis can determine, evenly distributed across geographies, economic classes, and demographic groups. The evidence for this is the consistent emergence of exceptional capability from every environment that creates the conditions to surface it.

The common failure at this stage is not absence of talent. It is the conflation of talent with demonstrated talent — with the evidence of talent that credentialed systems can recognise. A student who attended an Odia-medium government school in a district with inadequate educational infrastructure may have identical cognitive capacity to a student at a private school in Delhi. The Delhi student's talent is legible to the systems that evaluate it. The Kalahandi student's is not, yet.

**Where this link breaks:** When selection systems mistake credential for capability. When the proxies used to identify talent — degree, institution, language fluency, network connection — are correlated with access rather than with the underlying quality they're supposed to measure. The talent is present. The recognition system cannot see it.

**Application pointer:** In hiring, a brief built around credential requirements is a brief built around access, not talent. The search that requires a specific university pedigree or a specific company background is filtering for the environments that produced access to those institutions — not filtering for the capability the role requires.

## Stage 2: Access

Access is the gateway. It includes information (knowing the opportunity exists), networks (having a connection to someone who can open the door), systems (being able to navigate the application, the process, the unwritten requirements), and mentors (having someone who has done it before and can tell you how).

Access is where first-generation professionals most commonly encounter an invisible ceiling. The opportunity exists. They have the talent. But the specific knowledge of how to reach the opportunity — which firm to target, how to write the application, how to behave in the interview, who to ask for an introduction — is held in networks they are not part of.

This is the information asymmetry problem in its most consequential form. It is not that the information is secret. It is that the information is social — it travels through relationships, and if you are not in the relevant relationship network, you do not receive it.

**Where this link breaks:** When access is embedded in social capital that is not uniformly distributed. The student who has a parent who went to IIM knows what the preparation process looks like from someone who has done it. The student who does not has to reconstruct that knowledge from scratch, from less reliable sources, at greater personal cost.

**Application pointer:** Organisations that want to access deeper talent pools need to actively build information bridges — not just post jobs, but create pathways that reach people who would not otherwise receive the information. This is not charity. It is a sourcing strategy.

## Stage 3: Opportunity

The opportunity itself — the door that opens, or doesn't. The job offer. The scholarship. The meeting. The contract.

This stage gets the most attention and is the least frequently the actual problem. The failure to receive an opportunity, when talent and access are in place, is often a process failure rather than a substantive one. The candidate who reaches the interview but cannot code-switch into the register the interviewer expects. The proposal that arrives through the right network but uses language that doesn't match the evaluator's frame.

**Where this link breaks:** When the evaluation criteria are misaligned with the actual requirements of the role or opportunity. When the interviewer is evaluating cultural fit in a way that filters for familiarity rather than capability. When the process has structural friction — length, timing, cost — that disproportionately disadvantages certain candidates.

**Application pointer:** Every opportunity has an unofficial set of requirements that differs from the official one. The official requirement is the job description. The unofficial requirement is the specific presentation, communication style, and social register the evaluator will respond to. Being explicit about both — and testing whether the unofficial requirements are actually correlated with job performance — is how you reduce this failure mode.

## Stage 4: Mobility

Mobility is the conversion of opportunity into actual movement. Economic mobility: does income change? Geographic mobility: can the person physically relocate to where the opportunity is? Social mobility: do the networks, cultural capital, and institutional affiliations change?

This stage fails when the friction of conversion exceeds the value of the opportunity. The job offer in a city 1,500 kilometres away, for a salary that doesn't cover relocation costs and the loss of family support infrastructure, may represent real opportunity that cannot be converted into real mobility. The scholarship that requires full-time study without income support may be inaccessible to the person whose family depends on their contribution.

**Where this link breaks:** When the opportunity is real but the conversion cost is too high. This is the most frequently underestimated failure mode in social mobility interventions. The opportunity was created. The person had the talent and the access. The door opened. And then they couldn't afford to walk through it.

**Application pointer:** In executive search, this manifests when a strong candidate exists in the market but cannot take the role because the package doesn't account for their actual relocation, counter-offer, or life-stage constraints. Getting the offer right requires understanding what conversion actually costs for this specific person — not what the average candidate costs to convert.

## Stage 5: Prosperity

Prosperity is the compounding effect of mobility. The question is not just whether one person moved through the chain — but whether the movement creates conditions that make the chain shorter for the next person.

When a first-generation professional reaches a senior role and actively creates access for people from similar backgrounds, they are shortening the chain. When they accumulate economic capital that changes what their children start with, they are shortening it further. The chain doesn't just benefit the individual — it bends the starting conditions for the people who come after.

**Where this link breaks:** When mobility is individual and does not compound. When the person who made it through the chain does not look back. When the systems that produced the breakthrough do not learn from it and replicate it at scale.

**Application pointer:** The most durable outcomes are not produced by point-in-time interventions — they are produced by individuals who have moved through the chain and deliberately built the infrastructure that makes the chain shorter. Mentorship. Network opening. Explicit knowledge transfer. These compound in ways that one-off programmes do not.

## How to use this framework

The chain breaks at one link. Before intervening, locate the break. Throwing access at a talent recognition problem produces waste. Throwing opportunity at an access problem produces the same. Throwing mobility support at a process evaluation problem doesn't fix the evaluation.

Locate the break. Fix the link that's broken. Then verify that the adjacent links are holding.

Most systemic failures are not failures of all five links simultaneously. They are one or two specific breaks, repeated consistently across a population, producing outcomes that look like global disadvantage but are actually specific and fixable.

The framework is a diagnostic. Use it as one.
