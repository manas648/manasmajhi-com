Majhi Group did not start with an office, a local client base, or a network of investors I could walk across town to meet. It started in Junagarh, Kalahandi, a district with none of those things within reach. Everything about how the business is built, remote by default, global by necessity, sourcing talent outside the pipelines everyone else fishes from, traces back to that starting condition.

This is not the essay about Kalahandi's famine narrative or what the district actually looks like beneath the headlines. [That version exists](/kalahandi/the-kalahandi-most-people-never-see) and I stand by it. This is a narrower question: what does building an actual company, from a place with none of the standard infrastructure for building one, actually require?

## The business had to be placeless

Executive search, as an industry, has historically been a local game. Firms build reputations in specific cities, work specific corporate ecosystems, place candidates through relationships built over years of proximity. That model was never available to me. Junagarh has no corporate ecosystem to be proximate to.

So Majhi Group was built the only way it could be: entirely remote, sourcing and placing candidates across geographies, with no single market as its home base. This was not a calculated bet on the future of distributed work. It was the only shape the business could take, given where it started.

What turned out to be true, a few years in, is that the world moved toward this model anyway. Remote hiring, distributed executive search, global candidate pools, these became normal for the industry roughly the same time they stopped being a constraint unique to founders like me. The firms that had to retrofit themselves for distributed work spent years unlearning local habits. Majhi Group never had those habits to unlearn.

>> The business wasn't built remote as a strategy. It was built remote because the alternative required infrastructure that didn't exist where I was standing.

## Where I learned to look for talent

Growing up in Junagarh, I watched capable people go unrecognized constantly, not because they lacked skill, but because there was no system positioned to notice them. The credentialing infrastructure, the recruiter networks, the alumni pipelines that connect talent to opportunity in dense urban markets, none of it reached a district like Kalahandi. Capability existed. The infrastructure to convert it into visible opportunity did not.

That observation became a direct input into how Majhi Group sources candidates. Most executive search runs on warm networks and credentialed pipelines, the same universities, the same prior employers, the same visible signals repeating across candidate pools. It is efficient, and it systematically misses the people who are competent but were never inside the pipeline to begin with.

Sourcing outside those pipelines is slower. It requires evaluating capability directly rather than reading it off a resume's pedigree. But it also means finding candidates that competitor firms, running the same searches through the same networks, never surface. The discipline of looking past the credential for the capability underneath it is not something I learned in executive search training. I learned it watching who got overlooked in Junagarh.

## Building without inherited infrastructure

Every resource a business in a major hub takes for granted, warm investor introductions, a peer network of other founders, a local talent pool to hire the first employees from, had to be built from nothing, starting from Kalahandi. There was no local version of any of it to draw on.

What that forces, practically, is a specific kind of discipline. Every client relationship starts cold. Every hire is sourced through direct outreach rather than a referral network that already exists. Every piece of credibility has to be earned through delivered work, because there is no local reputation to borrow against.

This is slower in the early stages than building from a hub would be. It also means the business that results does not depend on any single market, relationship, or geography to function, because it was never allowed to depend on one in the first place.

>> Every resource had to be earned, not inherited. That constraint, uncomfortable at the time, is the reason the business doesn't depend on any single geography to survive.

## What actually got built

Majhi Group now operates globally, placing C-suite and VP-level candidates across markets, with a structure that was never rebuilt for distributed work because it was never built any other way. Majhi OS, the hiring infrastructure company built alongside it, extends the same premise: that the constraint of not being anywhere in particular can become an advantage, if the business is designed around it from the start rather than retrofitted later.

None of this required Kalahandi to change first. It required treating the absence of local infrastructure as a design constraint rather than a disqualifying condition. The place did not hand me the business. It handed me the specific shape the business had to take, and that shape turned out to travel well.
