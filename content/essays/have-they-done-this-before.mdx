Have They Done This Before?
For decades, hiring followed a simple rule.
Find someone who has already done the job.
Then hire them.
At first glance, it sounds logical.
If you need a VP of Engineering, hire someone who has been a VP of Engineering.
If you need a Head of Product, find someone who has already held that title.
If you need a leader to scale a business, hire someone who has already scaled a business.
The logic is straightforward.
Past experience reduces risk.
The problem is that the world is changing faster than experience can keep up.
Many organizations are still optimizing for yesterday's certainty while operating in tomorrow's
environment.
That creates a problem.
Some of the most important challenges businesses face today did not exist five years ago.
The tools are different.
The markets are different.
Customer expectations are different.
Technology is evolving at an extraordinary pace.
And yet hiring processes often remain unchanged.
The default question remains:
"Have they done this before?"
1

It is not a bad question.
It is simply an incomplete one.
Because the future rarely looks exactly like the past.
Imagine two candidates.
The first has spent ten years doing the exact same job in a stable environment.
The second has spent ten years repeatedly learning new industries, adapting to new technologies, and
solving unfamiliar problems.
Who is better prepared for a world that keeps changing?
The answer is not always obvious.
Experience matters.
But adaptability matters too.
Increasingly, I believe adaptability may become the more valuable asset.
Knowledge is becoming cheaper.
Access to information is becoming easier.
Artificial intelligence is making expertise more accessible than ever before.
What remains difficult is learning.
Unlearning.
Adjusting.
Navigating ambiguity.
Making decisions when no clear answer exists.
These are not knowledge problems.
They are adaptability problems.
The best people I have worked with share a common trait.
2

They learn quickly.
They are curious.
They are comfortable being uncomfortable.
They can move from certainty to uncertainty without losing momentum.
Most importantly, they do not define themselves by what they already know.
They define themselves by what they can figure out.
That distinction matters.
Because the organizations that thrive over the next decade will not simply be the ones with the most
experienced employees.
They will be the ones with the most adaptable employees.
This is especially true in leadership roles.
Executives are rarely hired to repeat the past.
They are hired to create the future.
A company that is growing rapidly often faces challenges nobody inside the organization has solved before.
A market leader may suddenly find itself disrupted.
A startup may need to evolve from survival mode to scale mode.
The question is no longer:
"Have they done this before?"
The question becomes:
"Can they figure out what comes next?"
That is a much harder question to answer.
It requires looking beyond titles.
Beyond résumés.
3

Beyond credentials.
It requires understanding how someone thinks.
How they learn.
How they make decisions.
How they respond when the playbook no longer works.
Those qualities are harder to measure.
They are also becoming more valuable.
The irony is that experience and adaptability are not opposites.
The strongest candidates often possess both.
They have enough experience to recognize patterns.
And enough adaptability to recognize when the old patterns no longer apply.
That combination is powerful.
As knowledge becomes increasingly commoditized, learning becomes increasingly valuable.
As expertise becomes easier to access, judgment becomes more important.
As change accelerates, adaptability becomes a competitive advantage.
The future does not belong exclusively to specialists.
Nor does it belong exclusively to generalists.
It belongs to people who can continuously evolve.
People who can learn, adapt, and create value in environments that did not exist when their careers began.
Perhaps that is the question more organizations should be asking.
Not:
"Have they done this before?"
But:
4

"Can they figure out what comes next?"
Learning Outcome
Past experience reduces risk, but adaptability creates future value. In a rapidly changing world, the ability to
learn may become more important than the knowledge someone already possesses.
