On 29 October 1999, the Super Cyclone took nearly 10,000 lives in Odisha. Twenty-five years later, when Cyclone Dana approached the same coastline, 810,896 people were evacuated, and the state reported zero human casualties.

Between these two moments lies a story not just of survival, but of a society that learnt to prepare, protect one another and face the future together.

The real change was not that cyclones became less dangerous. It was that Odisha became better at turning warnings into action — and action into collective responsibility.

## The night we will never forget

In October 1999, Prasanta Haldar was fifteen years old.

He lived in Dahibar, a village in Jagatsinghpur. When the Super Cyclone struck, he lost his father, mother and two younger sisters. He survived after being swept away and was eventually found at a hospital in Erasama.

Today, Prasanta is married, has two children and earns his living as a fisherman. He returned to the sea. The same sea that took his family became the source from which he rebuilt his life.

His story offers a glimpse of what the people of Odisha endured on 29 October 1999. With estimated maximum wind speeds reaching 260–270 km/h in its core area, the Super Cyclone devastated the state's coastline. The official death toll was 9,885. Thousands of homes were destroyed. Entire villages were devastated. Families lost parents, children, siblings and everything they had built over generations.

For many, survival itself was an act of courage. For those who survived, the struggle to begin again was only just starting.

Odisha remembers that day as Black Friday.

But the story of Odisha cannot end with the tragedy of that night.

## When the survivors became protectors

Something extraordinary happened in the years that followed. A people who had experienced unimaginable loss began preparing not just for their own safety, but for the safety of their neighbours.

This transformation was not accidental. It required institutions to learn from failure, governments to invest in disaster preparedness, forecasters to improve warnings, and communities to become active participants in protecting lives.

Cyclone shelters were built. Warning systems improved. Evacuation plans were strengthened. Disaster-response institutions and village-level committees became more capable. Communities began organising themselves, and ordinary people became an essential part of the effort.

Perhaps the most powerful illustration of this change is found in T. Alia from Sandhakuda. The 1999 Super Cyclone had taken two of his sons and his daughter. Fourteen years later, when Cyclone Phailin approached Odisha, Alia helped evacuate hundreds of people from the seaside. He remembered his children as he helped others leave.

A man who had known the deepest personal loss was now helping other families avoid the same fate.

The people who had once needed saving had become the people who went out to save others.

>> That is perhaps the truest measure of Odisha's transformation.

## Learning to stand together

In October 2013, Cyclone Phailin approached the Odisha coast. Nearly 9.84 lakh people were evacuated in the state before landfall, and 21 deaths were reported in Odisha.

It was a remarkable achievement, made possible by years of preparation, better forecasting, public awareness, institutional coordination and growing trust between communities and those responsible for their safety.

The real change was visible in how people responded. Women from self-help groups persuaded fishermen not to venture into the sea. Local volunteers went from house to house. Village leaders and ordinary residents worked together to ensure that warnings reached the people who needed them most.

Preparedness was no longer simply something done for communities. It had become something communities took part in.

The learning continued. When Cyclone Dana approached in October 2024, the Ministry of Home Affairs recorded 810,896 people evacuated in Odisha. The National Disaster Response Force subsequently reported that no casualty was caused by the cyclone in the state.

Dana was considerably weaker than the 1999 Super Cyclone and less intense than Phailin. The storms are not comparable in intensity. But the response to Dana demonstrated how far Odisha had travelled in its ability to anticipate danger, mobilise resources and move vulnerable populations to safety.

The numbers help put that progress in perspective:

**The 1999 Super Cyclone:** Estimated maximum winds of 260–270 km/h in the core area. The official death toll was 9,885.

**Cyclone Phailin, 2013:** Estimated winds of 210–220 km/h near landfall. Nearly 9.84 lakh people were evacuated in Odisha; 21 deaths were reported in the state.

**Cyclone Dana, 2024:** Landfall winds of 100–110 km/h, gusting to 120 km/h. More than 8.1 lakh people were evacuated in Odisha, with no human casualties reported from the cyclone.

These wind figures are approximate and come from different reporting contexts: the 1999 figure describes maximum winds in the storm's core, while the later figures describe winds around landfall. They should not be treated as a like-for-like measure of storm intensity.

The more important measure of progress is the ability to anticipate danger, communicate warnings, organise evacuations and ensure that people act on those warnings.

Consider one family in the coastal settlements of Satabhaya and Magarkandha.

Before Dana, Jagabandhu Behera and other residents had to cross the Bausagali River to reach safer shelters, walking about ten kilometres along muddy roads. His wife, Manorama, was reluctant to leave behind their 20 goats and two cows. These were not merely animals. They were part of the family's livelihood and security.

Eventually, they penned the goats, left the cows loose and moved to safety. It was not an easy decision, but they made it.

That small, deeply human moment says something important about preparedness. It is not just about building shelters or issuing warnings. It is about people understanding the danger, trusting one another and finding the courage to act, even when it comes at a personal cost.

A community becomes resilient when people look beyond their immediate circumstances and take responsibility for one another.

## The work is not over

It would be easy to end this story with the zero casualties reported during Dana. Odisha has every reason to take pride in that achievement. But survival is only the beginning of recovery.

A fisherman who returns to a damaged boat, a farmer whose fields have been ruined by saltwater, a family that has lost its home: each still faces an uncertain future.

Saving lives is essential. Restoring livelihoods is equally important.

Disaster management cannot end when a storm passes. It must continue until families can rebuild their homes, restore their livelihoods and regain their economic security.

Coastal communities need stronger homes, secure livelihoods, protected mangroves and reliable support long after a cyclone has passed. Families who lose their assets must not be left to rebuild alone. Promises made to survivors must be honoured.

There will be more storms and new challenges. Preparedness must remain a continuing commitment, not an achievement we celebrate once and forget.

>> A state is not resilient simply because it can evacuate people. It is resilient when it can help them rebuild their lives afterwards.

## This is our story

The sea is still there, and the cyclones will come again. Generations of Odias will continue to live, work and build their lives along this coastline. But something fundamental has changed.

We have learnt to prepare, to listen, and to treat protecting a neighbour as just as important as protecting ourselves. We have also learnt that even after unimaginable loss, life can begin again.

Prasanta returned to the sea. T. Alia turned his grief into an act of courage. Jagabandhu and Manorama chose to leave their animals and move towards safety. Their stories are different, but together they say something about the character of a people.

This is not a story about people who were never broken. It is about people who refused to let their losses define their future. It is a story every Odia, wherever they may live, can recognise and take pride in.

**We may bend before the storm, we may lose everything, and we may have to begin again, but we will always stand together. That is Odisha. That is our spirit.**
