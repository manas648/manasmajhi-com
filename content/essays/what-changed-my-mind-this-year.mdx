What Changed My Mind This Year?
One of the most valuable habits I have developed is paying attention to ideas that no longer survive contact
with reality.
Not because I enjoy being wrong.
Nobody does.
But because growth often begins where certainty ends.
Every year, I try to identify beliefs that have evolved.
Assumptions that no longer hold.
Ideas that became more nuanced.
Perspectives that expanded.
This year was no different.
Several things changed my mind.
Not completely.
But meaningfully.
And those changes taught me more than many of the things I got right.
1. Talent Is Not The Biggest Constraint
For a long time, I believed talent was the primary differentiator.
The more people I met, the less convinced I became.
Talent is everywhere.
Intelligence is everywhere.
Ambition is everywhere.
What is not evenly distributed is opportunity.
1

Access.
Networks.
Visibility.
Mentorship.
Information.
Two people with similar abilities can experience radically different outcomes because one gained access
earlier than the other.
That realization changed how I think about development, hiring, education, and entrepreneurship.
The challenge is often not finding talent.
The challenge is creating pathways.
2. Systems Matter More Than Individuals
When I was younger, I was fascinated by exceptional individuals.
Visionaries.
Leaders.
Entrepreneurs.
Builders.
I still admire them.
But I now pay far more attention to systems.
Because systems determine what becomes possible at scale.
A great person can change a company.
A great system can change thousands of companies.
A great teacher can change a classroom.
A great education system can change generations.
2

The larger the challenge, the more important systems become.
This shift in thinking influences everything I build today.
3. Understanding Power Is Different From Chasing Power
A year ago, I knew very little about influence, elite networks, and how power moves through institutions.
Today, I understand much more.
And strangely, that understanding has made me less interested in chasing it.
Not because power lacks value.
Because power is a tool.
Not a destination.
The most impressive people I met were not necessarily the most powerful.
They were often the most grounded.
The most thoughtful.
The most disciplined.
The most comfortable with themselves.
That distinction stayed with me.
4. Geography Matters Less Than It Used To
Growing up, geography felt decisive.
Some places appeared full of opportunity.
Others appeared distant from it.
Technology has changed that.
Not completely.
3

But significantly.
Today, someone in Odisha can work with clients in New York.
A student in a small town can access world-class knowledge.
A founder outside traditional hubs can build global businesses.
The barriers have not disappeared.
But they are lower than they have ever been.
That realization has made me increasingly optimistic.
5. Long-Term Thinking Is A Competitive Advantage
This belief strengthened rather than changed.
Every year provides more evidence.
Most people dramatically underestimate compounding.
Not because they lack intelligence.
Because compounding is difficult to observe in real time.
The first year often looks insignificant.
The fifth year looks different.
The tenth year looks transformational.
This applies to skills.
Relationships.
Businesses.
Reputations.
Ideas.
The people who stay committed long enough often appear lucky from the outside.
4

What others call luck is frequently the visible result of invisible consistency.
6. Progress Is More Important Than Perfection
Earlier in my career, I spent more energy trying to get things right.
Today, I spend more energy trying to move things forward.
Perfection feels productive.
Progress is productive.
The distinction matters.
Many opportunities are lost waiting for ideal conditions.
Many successes emerge from iterative improvement.
Action creates feedback.
Feedback creates learning.
Learning creates progress.
The cycle matters more than the starting point.
7. Opportunity Is The Real Lever
If there is one idea that became clearer this year than any other, it is this:
Opportunity changes lives.
Not motivation.
Not inspiration.
Not slogans.
Opportunity.
A single opportunity can alter a trajectory.
5

A career.
A business.
A family.
A future.
The more I observe the world, the more convinced I become that creating opportunities is one of the
highest-leverage activities available.
That belief now sits at the center of how I think about work.
And increasingly, how I think about life.
Looking Forward
The older I get, the less interested I become in appearing certain.
And the more interested I become in staying curious.
Reality is complicated.
People are complicated.
Systems are complicated.
The goal is not to have all the answers.
The goal is to continue improving the quality of the questions.
Because good questions tend to lead somewhere useful.
This year changed my mind about many things.
Hopefully next year does too.
Because if nothing changes your mind, you are probably not learning enough.
Learning Outcome
Growth often comes from updating assumptions rather than defending them. The willingness to change
your mind in response to new evidence is one of the most valuable forms of learning.
