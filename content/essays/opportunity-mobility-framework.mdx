There is a version of the opportunity problem that the Opportunity Framework addresses: the chain that must exist before a door can open. Market intelligence, networks, access systems, the infrastructure of reaching an opportunity.

This framework addresses a different problem: the door is open. The opportunity is real. And the person still cannot walk through it.

The Opportunity Mobility Framework maps the four conditions that must be true simultaneously for an opportunity to convert into actual mobility. All four are necessary. Each can fail independently. And when any one fails, the door being open is irrelevant.

## The four conditions

```
OPPORTUNITY
  ↓
  [The door exists.
   The role, the programme, the path is real
   and available to this person.]

CAPABILITY
  ↓
  [The person can walk through it.
   Skills, preparation, confidence, and
   the specific readiness this door requires.]

PARTICIPATION
  ↓
  [The person does walk through it.
   No structural barrier blocks entry —
   cost, timing, geography, family, bias.]

MOBILITY
  [The outcome materialises.
   Economic, social, professional change
   that compounds over time.]
```

## Condition 1: Opportunity

The opportunity must actually exist — not theoretically, not in aggregate, but specifically for this person at this moment.

This condition fails more often than it should, even in environments that have nominally created opportunity. A scholarship that exists but is not advertised in the language or channel that reaches the target population. A job opening that is posted but is filled through a referral network before applications close. A programme that is available but requires documentation that a specific demographic is systematically less likely to have.

The gap between "the opportunity exists" and "the opportunity is accessible to this person" is real and frequently underestimated.

**Where this condition fails:** When availability is measured at the system level rather than the individual level. When the opportunity is technically open but practically reaches only the people who were already well-positioned.

**Application pointer:** For any opportunity you are trying to make available, audit the actual path from the target person to the opportunity. Not the intended path — the actual path. Where does it require information they don't have? A connection they're missing? Documentation they can't produce?

## Condition 2: Capability

Capability is what the person brings to the door. Skills. Knowledge. Preparation. Confidence. And the specific, often unspoken, social readiness that a particular opportunity requires.

The capability condition has an official layer and an unofficial layer. The official layer is what the opportunity says it requires — qualifications, experience, demonstrated competencies. The unofficial layer is what it actually rewards: the ability to communicate in the register the evaluator finds credible, the cultural literacy that makes the interaction feel comfortable, the specific kind of confidence that the selection process favours.

First-generation professionals frequently clear the official capability bar and fail at the unofficial one — not because they are less capable, but because the unofficial layer was built for a specific kind of person and they are not that person. The interview went well technically but they didn't feel right. The application was strong but the statement didn't have the right voice.

**Where this condition fails:** When capability is evaluated primarily through its official markers and the unofficial requirements are never made explicit. When the person has the underlying capability but lacks the translation layer — the ability to present it in the form the evaluator can receive.

**Application pointer:** Every opportunity has a capability gap between what is officially required and what is actually evaluated. Making that gap explicit — in preparation programmes, in hiring processes, in access interventions — is how you prevent the unofficial layer from functioning as a hidden filter.

## Condition 3: Participation

Participation is whether the person who has the opportunity and the capability actually enters the process. And this is the condition that fails most silently.

The person who doesn't apply because the application cost — in time, money, or social risk — is too high relative to the uncertain return. The person who withdraws because the first interaction with the process made them feel like they didn't belong. The person who was selected and then couldn't physically get to the location. The person who couldn't take the unpaid internship because they needed income. The person who couldn't attend the networking event because of childcare.

Structural barriers to participation are not always visible to the people who design the opportunity. They are very visible to the people encountering them.

**Where this condition fails:** When the cost of participation is not modelled. When withdrawal is attributed to lack of interest rather than structural friction. When the process is designed for people with certain resources — time, money, flexibility, geographic proximity — and does not accommodate those who lack them.

**Application pointer:** Map the friction. For each step between "opportunity exists" and "person is inside the process," ask: what does this step cost in time, money, and social exposure? Who cannot afford that cost? The answer to the last question defines who the opportunity is actually available to, regardless of who it is nominally aimed at.

## Condition 4: Mobility

The fourth condition is whether the opportunity converts into actual change. Not whether the person got the job — whether the job changed their economic position, their social networks, their options, and their children's starting conditions.

This is where a large class of well-intentioned interventions fail at the final step. The person got into the programme. They completed it. They received the credential or the placement. And then the mobility didn't materialise — because the programme placed them in a role that didn't pay enough to change their economic trajectory, or in a company that didn't give them the network access the opportunity was supposed to produce, or in a position that didn't convert into career progression because the informal mentorship and sponsorship that drives promotion was not available to them.

Getting through the door is not the same as mobility. Mobility requires that what's on the other side of the door is genuinely transformative for this person's trajectory — not just that they passed through it.

**Where this condition fails:** When outcomes are measured at entry (did they get the job?) rather than at impact (did the job change their trajectory?). When the mobility intervention ends at placement without tracking whether the placement produced actual mobility. When the organisation that created the opportunity considers its work done when the person started, not when their trajectory changed.

**Application pointer:** Define mobility in advance, not after. What specific change in economic position, career options, or social capital constitutes success for this person? Measure against that definition at 12 months, not at day one.

## Why most interventions get this wrong

The standard intervention model focuses heavily on Condition 1 (creating the opportunity) and partially on Condition 2 (preparing the person). It rarely addresses Condition 3 (removing barriers to participation) and almost never tracks Condition 4 (whether mobility actually resulted).

This produces the pattern that is visible across social mobility programmes globally: high rates of nominal access, large dropout rates at participation, and impact studies that struggle to demonstrate lasting trajectory change.

The framework is not a critique of effort. Most of these programmes are run by people who care deeply and work hard. The critique is of the diagnostic model — the assumption that creating opportunity is sufficient, when in reality it is one of four necessary conditions.

All four must hold. Simultaneously. For this specific person. At this specific moment.

That is a harder problem than creating the door. But it is the actual problem.
