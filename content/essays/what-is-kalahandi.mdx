Kalahandi is a district in the southwestern corner of Odisha, bordered by Chhattisgarh to the north, Nabarangpur and Nuapada to the west, and Rayagada and Kandhamal to the east and south. Its geography is shaped by the Eastern Ghats — a forested, hilly terrain that makes the place visually distinct from the flatter coastal regions that define Odisha in most people's mental maps of the state.

Most people who have heard of Kalahandi encountered the name through one source: the famine coverage of the 1980s. That coverage was real and the conditions it documented were real. Kalahandi became shorthand in Indian policy discourse for underdevelopment at its most visible — a place that made the nightly news because hunger had become severe enough to be undeniable.

The name stuck. Decades later, Kalahandi is still introduced in many contexts through that frame. It is a frame that is both accurate about a specific historical moment and wrong about the place.

## The actual geography

The landscape itself is striking. The Indravati river runs through the district before flowing west into Chhattisgarh. The Tel river originates here. Forest cover is dense in significant portions of the district — the kind of forest that contains biodiversity the coastal world barely registers. The hills create microclimates that support agriculture that flat-land models do not account for.

Bauxite deposits sit in the hills. Iron ore. The mineral base that makes the district interesting to industry — and that has created its own complex history of extraction agreements, environmental disputes, and the question of who actually benefits when resources below the ground become resources on someone's balance sheet.

The agricultural land, when properly irrigated, is fertile. Rice, pulses, oilseeds. The farmers here have generations of knowledge about their specific soils, their specific rainfall patterns, their specific growing windows. That knowledge is not in any database. It exists in practice, transmitted through proximity and observation.

## The people

The population is a mix of communities — Scheduled Tribes form a significant portion, including the Kondh, Gond, and other indigenous groups. Their presence predates any administrative category. Their relationship to the land, the forest, and the local economy is deeper and more complex than any policy framework has captured.

There are also the communities that have lived alongside them for generations — traders, farmers, administrators, teachers, people who built lives in a place that most of India was simultaneously ignoring and catastrophizing.

The people I grew up around had a quality I have struggled to name precisely. Directness, maybe. A matter-of-factness about difficulty that does not tip into resignation and does not perform struggle for outside observers. They were solving problems they had, with what they had, without waiting for a development intervention to arrive and tell them what to do next.

## What the data captures and what it doesn't

By any standard development metric — income, infrastructure, literacy, infant mortality — Kalahandi has historically underperformed against national averages. These metrics are not wrong. The underperformance is real.

What the metrics do not capture: the agricultural knowledge that has no institutional recognition. The informal credit systems that function precisely because they are not designed by people who have never been to Kalahandi. The community structures that have outlasted every program aimed at replacing them.

What the metrics do not capture: what it feels like to be from a place that the outside world has defined by its worst decade, and to know, from direct experience, that the place is far larger and more complex than that definition.

## Why I write about it

I write about Kalahandi because I am from it — not from the district itself, but from the region it shapes, the proximity that makes it part of how I see everything else.

And I write about it because the pattern I learned by growing up near Kalahandi — resources that exist, systems that fail to convert them, the gap between potential and outcome — is the same pattern I see in executive search, in company building, in how institutions work and fail everywhere.

The specific geography matters. The lesson is not local.
