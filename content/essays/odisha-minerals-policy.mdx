Odisha sits on some of the most significant mineral deposits in Asia. Iron ore reserves that rank among the largest globally. The biggest chromite deposits on the continent. Substantial bauxite in the hills of Koraput, Kalahandi, and Keonjhar. Significant coal, manganese, and nickel. The state contributes roughly 24% of India's steel production and is the source of raw materials for industrial operations across eastern India.

It is also one of the lower-ranked Indian states on per capita income.

This gap — between the extraordinary value of what lies beneath Odisha's surface and the ordinary development outcomes visible above it — is the most important economic policy question in the state. Understanding it requires distinguishing between three things that are often conflated: mineral extraction, mineral processing, and mineral-led development.

## The Extraction-Development Paradox

Resource economics has a long record of studying what researchers call the "resource curse" — the empirical pattern in which regions and countries with significant natural resource wealth tend to develop more slowly than those without it. The explanations are multiple: dependence on a single revenue source creates volatility; resource revenues can reduce the pressure for broader tax base development and institutional improvement; powerful interests in the extraction sector can crowd out investment in other sectors; and environmental degradation from extraction can impose long-term costs that outweigh short-term royalty revenues.

Odisha is not a textbook case of the resource curse — the state has made genuine development progress over the past two decades, and mineral revenues have funded public investment that has improved infrastructure and services across the state. But the paradox is real: Odisha's mineral endowment has not produced the kind of industrial ecosystem that would make the state's development trajectory self-sustaining rather than dependent on continued extraction.

The core reason is value addition, or the lack of it.

## How Value Is Created and Captured

Consider iron ore. The ore comes out of the ground in Odisha at a value of perhaps Rs.2,000-4,000 per tonne, depending on grade. It leaves the state by rail to steel plants in Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, or other states, where it is processed into pellets, then sponge iron or pig iron, then steel billets, then finished steel products. By the time it reaches the end user as structural steel or automotive steel or consumer goods, the value per tonne of original ore content is perhaps 15-20 times what it was at the pit head.

The state of Odisha captures the royalty — a percentage of the pit head value. It does not capture the value added in processing, in fabrication, in final use. That value accrues to the states and companies where processing and fabrication happen.

The same logic applies to bauxite. The ore has one value. Refined to alumina, it has roughly 4-5 times that value. Smelted to aluminium, perhaps 10 times. Fabricated into finished products — aerospace components, automotive parts, packaging material — perhaps 20-30 times. Odisha has significant bauxite but has historically captured only the ore-stage value in most of its bauxite production.

This is not a mistake that is unique to Odisha. It is the standard pattern in mineral-rich developing regions globally. The question is whether policy can change it, and what the constraints on doing so are.

## What Odisha Has Done

Odisha has pursued a strategy of using mineral access as an incentive to attract industrial processing within the state. The approach is logical: if a steel company has secure access to iron ore in Odisha, it has an incentive to locate its steel plant in Odisha rather than shipping ore elsewhere.

Several significant industrial developments reflect this strategy. The Rourkela Steel Plant — established in the 1950s as part of India's early industrialization push — was located in Odisha because of its proximity to iron ore and coal. It remains one of Odisha's largest industrial employers. NALCO's aluminium operations in Angul represent a successful instance of bauxite-to-aluminium value addition within the state. The Vedanta alumina refinery at Lanjigarh was designed to process Kalahandi and Koraput bauxite into alumina within Odisha.

The state has also signed numerous MoUs with industrial groups — for steel plants, aluminium smelters, and downstream processing facilities. The conversion rate of MoUs into operating plants has been low, for reasons that include land acquisition challenges, delayed environmental clearances, infrastructure gaps (particularly power and water), and the normal attrition that affects large industrial projects globally.

## The Niyamgiri Case and Its Implications

The most consequential single event in Odisha's recent mineral policy history is the Niyamgiri decision — the 2013 Supreme Court ruling that required the gram sabhas of the Dongria Kondh tribal communities to vote on whether bauxite mining of the Niyamgiri range in Kalahandi could proceed, on the grounds that it would affect their religious and cultural rights.

Twelve gram sabhas voted. All twelve rejected the mining proposal. Mining has not proceeded.

The decision has implications well beyond Niyamgiri. It established that tribal gram sabhas have a meaningful right to veto resource extraction in their territories when cultural and religious rights are implicated — a landmark in tribal rights law, widely hailed by environmental and community rights advocates. For mineral investors, it also introduced greater uncertainty about the security of concessions in areas with significant tribal populations, which describes a large proportion of Odisha's mineral belt.

The policy tension this creates is genuine and not easily resolved. Tribal communities have real and historically underprotected rights to land, forest, and cultural continuity. The state also has real interests in developing its mineral wealth in ways that produce public revenue and employment. These interests conflict in specific locations and require negotiated solutions that have not yet been systematized.

The broader lesson of Niyamgiri is that mineral development in tribal areas requires genuine community engagement and benefit sharing — not as a compliance exercise but as a precondition for the social legitimacy that makes sustained development possible. Projects that proceed over community objection tend to produce ongoing resistance, legal challenges, and social costs that reduce or eliminate the economic rationale for the project.

## The Value Addition Imperative

The most important long-run policy direction for Odisha's mineral sector is straightforward even if it is technically and politically difficult: more processing, fabrication, and value addition within the state before mineral outputs leave Odisha.

For iron ore: pelletization plants (converting ore fines to pellets suitable for steel production) represent a relatively accessible first step that has been taken in some locations. Moving further up the chain to sponge iron, steel, and downstream products requires larger capital commitments, more stable power supply, and more sophisticated industrial infrastructure.

For bauxite: the NALCO model — mine, refine to alumina, smelt to aluminium, within Odisha — is the template. Replicating it or expanding it requires resolving the bauxite access constraints that have significantly limited output at Vedanta's Lanjigarh refinery since the 2013 Niyamgiri ruling and requires attracting additional aluminium producers.

For chromite: Odisha's chromite is a globally significant resource. Ferrochrome smelting within Odisha (currently done in some operations) represents value addition over raw chromite export. Stainless steel production, which uses ferrochrome, represents a further step.

## The Fiscal Dimension

Mineral royalties and related revenues are a significant component of Odisha's state budget — roughly 25-30% of the state's own tax and non-tax revenue in recent years, depending on commodity prices and production volumes. This dependence creates fiscal vulnerability: when iron ore prices fall or production slows due to regulatory delays, the state's revenue position weakens.

The District Mineral Foundation (DMF), established under the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act 2015, requires that a percentage of royalties be deposited into a district-level fund for the benefit of communities affected by mining. Odisha has implemented this through the Pradhan Mantri Khanij Kshetra Kalyan Yojana (PMKKKY). The actual disbursement and impact of DMF funds has been variable across districts — a governance challenge rather than a policy one.

Odisha has also been a proactive state in pursuing higher royalty rates and in the e-auction of mineral blocks, which has improved transparency in mineral concession allocation and increased state revenues compared to the era of discretionary allocation.

## What Good Mineral Policy Looks Like

The states that have most successfully converted mineral wealth into development — Norway's oil fund model at the extreme end; Botswana's diamond-to-development story more modestly — share several characteristics.

They save a portion of mineral revenues in dedicated funds during boom periods rather than spending everything in real time, which reduces volatility and creates an endowment for the post-mineral period.

They invest mineral revenues in sectors that compound value over time — infrastructure, education, health — rather than in current consumption.

They build the industrial policy and infrastructure (particularly power, rail, and water) that enables value-addition processing to happen within their borders.

And they develop governance frameworks for managing the social and environmental costs of extraction that maintain community legitimacy and avoid the compounding conflicts that eventually make extraction politically impossible.

Odisha is further along this path than its position fifteen years ago and less far along than it should be given the scale of the resource.

## The Perspective from Kalahandi

I grew up in Kalahandi, which sits on bauxite reserves that are among the most significant in India. The district is on the national development priority list. The Vedanta refinery at Lanjigarh was built in part because of Kalahandi's bauxite. The Niyamgiri controversy played out in the district's most southern block.

What I observe from that vantage point is that the conversation about Odisha's minerals is still primarily a conversation about extraction — how much to extract, from where, under what environmental and social conditions, with what royalty rates. The conversation about value addition — how to ensure that the multiple steps between ore and finished product happen within Odisha and within the districts where the ore is located — is less developed.

The fiscal math is clear. A tonne of iron ore leaves Odisha and enters supply chains that produce finished goods selling for many multiples of its extraction value — with the iron content in a typical vehicle representing a value multiplication of 100x or more by the time it reaches the consumer. The state captures royalties on the tonne. The vehicle manufacturer captures margins that represent many multiples of the ore's value. Getting more of that manufacturing chain within Odisha would transform the state's development trajectory more fundamentally than increasing extraction volume or royalty rates.

This is not an original observation. It is the standard conclusion of resource economics. The difficulty is implementation — the industrial policy, the infrastructure, the community relations, the governance that turns the conclusion into practice. That is where the work is, and it is a harder and slower task than announcing another MoU.
