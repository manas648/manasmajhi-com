In five years of executive search, I have lost count of how many times I presented a candidate who was not on the hiring manager's radar — someone who hadn't surfaced through the usual channels, whose name wasn't familiar, whose LinkedIn profile didn't announce what they were capable of — and watched that person become the hire the client called, a year later, the best decision they made.

The talent was always there. The discovery infrastructure wasn't.

There is a persistent assumption in how organisations think about talent: that it surfaces. That the most capable people become visible because their work speaks for itself, and that the promotion, recognition, and opportunity that follow are a roughly accurate reflection of who has actually earned them.

This assumption is wrong in ways that have significant consequences — for the individuals who are underrecognised, for the organisations that miss their contribution, and for the broader question of whether our hiring and promotion systems are producing the outcomes they're supposed to produce.

## Why visibility and capability diverge

**Talent is domain-specific; visibility is social.** The things that make someone excellent at their work are not the same things that make them noticed by the people with the power to reward that excellence. A brilliant analyst who produces insights that drive better decisions may be far less visible than a mediocre executive who speaks well in large meetings. The analyst's contribution is real but diffuse — it's felt in the quality of decisions downstream, not in the room where recognition happens. The executive's contribution is visible in exactly the moments when visibility is being assessed.

**The feedback that rewards excellence is often indirect.** Great work in many roles — deep technical work, operational excellence, the kind of thinking that prevents problems from occurring rather than solving them after the fact — produces outcomes that are hard to attribute. The system that didn't fail because someone anticipated and prevented the failure looks identical to the system that didn't fail because nothing went wrong. The person whose work prevented the failure is invisible in a way the person who fixed the crisis is not.

Prevention is always undervalued relative to rescue. This is true in engineering systems. It is equally true in organisations.

**Self-promotion is a skill that is unevenly distributed, and unevenly rewarded.** The ability to make work visible, to advocate for your own contribution, to position yourself in conversations where decisions about recognition are being made — these are skills that are taught, modelled, and rewarded in some environments and penalised in others. They are also skills that are culturally mediated: what counts as appropriate self-advocacy varies by culture, by gender norms, by class background, and by organisational context.

People who grew up in environments where speaking about your own accomplishments was considered inappropriate — or who come from cultural backgrounds where deference is a social norm — often enter professional environments with a significant disadvantage in visibility that has nothing to do with their capability. I have placed candidates from Tier 2 Indian cities who outperformed their more visible peers from metros in every substantive measure of the role, but who lost interview processes where self-narration skill was being tested as a proxy for leadership ability.

**Geography has historically constrained visibility.** The talented person in a second-tier city, or a country that is not a global financial centre, has historically had less access to the networks, platforms, and institutions through which professional visibility is built. This is changing — remote work and digital platforms have reduced the visibility premium of being in the right place — but it hasn't changed entirely. The most powerful professional networks are still geographically concentrated, and the warm introduction still travels faster through shared physical proximity than through LinkedIn.

**Sponsorship is not equally distributed.** One of the most consistent findings in research on career advancement is that who advocates for you — who speaks your name in rooms you're not in, who recommends you for opportunities, who signals to decision-makers that you're worth taking a risk on — matters enormously. Research finds that only [23% of employees report having access to a sponsor](https://www.mentoringcomplete.com/what-are-workplace-sponsorship-programs-and-why-we-need-them/), and women are [20% less likely than men to have a strategic network of sponsors](https://seramount.com/articles/three-ways-formal-sponsorship-programs-advance-diverse-talent/). And sponsorship is not randomly distributed. It tends to flow along lines of similarity: shared background, shared networks, shared reference points. People who are unlike the people making decisions about them — in demographic background, cultural context, in class background, in educational pedigree — are less likely to have sponsors, and therefore less likely to be visible in the moments when visibility counts.

>> Talent and visibility are different variables, determined by different things. The system that didn't fail because someone anticipated and prevented the failure looks identical to the system that didn't fail because nothing went wrong. Prevention is invisible in a way that rescue is not.

## The cost to organisations

Organisations that systematically miss capable but unnoticed talent pay a real cost. They over-promote people who are excellent at making themselves visible and under-promote people who are excellent at doing the work. Over time, this produces leadership that is selected for self-promotion ability rather than the capabilities that actually drive outcomes. It also creates an environment that drives away the people who are excellent at producing results but unwilling or unable to compete on visibility.

The executive search and hiring process is particularly vulnerable to this failure. Candidates who present with confidence, who tell their story well, who have the social infrastructure that produces strong networks and visible track records, tend to outperform in hiring processes relative to their actual capability. Candidates who are quieter, from less networked backgrounds, who have done excellent work in roles that don't have obvious visibility, tend to underperform in hiring processes relative to their actual capability.

This gap is expensive. The confident presenter who was hired because the process was optimised for confidence is not the same as the rigorous operator who would have produced better outcomes. Organizations that run processes optimised for presentation skill and then wonder why their senior hires underperform have not diagnosed the problem correctly.

## What reference checks actually correct

Reference checks, done rigorously, are one of the most powerful correctives to the visibility bias of interviews. The people who have actually worked alongside a candidate — who have seen them in unscripted moments, who have observed their behaviour when no one is evaluating them, who know what happened when something went wrong — give a view of capability that is significantly less contaminated by self-presentation skill.

The candidate who underperforms in an interview relative to their actual capability almost always performs better in rigorous reference checks. This is one of the most consistent patterns I have seen across five years of executive search. The candidate who is smooth in the interview but mediocre in references presents the opposite pattern.

This is why I invest more in reference process than most search firms do. Two-hour conversations with people who worked directly alongside the candidate, conducted after the formal interview, after the first impression has been formed, focused on specific observable behaviour rather than general character assessments — this is where the capability that interviews miss becomes visible. The reference check is not a verification exercise. It is a discovery exercise.

## What organisations can do

Three interventions have evidence behind them.

**Structured sponsorship programs.** Mentoring is not the same as sponsorship. A sponsor does not just advise — they use their own credibility to advocate for someone in rooms that person is not in. Organisations that create deliberate sponsorship relationships, rather than relying on the informal networks that reproduce existing patterns of advantage, have better outcomes on diversity and on identifying hidden talent than those that leave sponsorship to emerge organically.

**Process design that reduces the weight of self-presentation.** Work samples. Blind assessment of output. Reference checks conducted rigorously before the final interview rather than as post-decision verification. Trial projects for senior roles. The interventions that reduce the weight of "how well did they perform in the interview" relative to "what do we actually know about what they produce" produce different and typically better hiring outcomes.

**Tracking advancement outcomes by group.** Organisations that measure who is advancing, at what rate, and relative to what evidence, can identify where visibility-based selection is distorting results. The organisation that promotes people who are demographically similar to existing leadership without examining whether the process is fair has not created a meritocracy. It has created a system that reproduces what already exists and calls it merit.

## What individuals can do

The practical implication for capable people who are not visible is that visibility is a skill that can be deliberately developed — and that developing it is not a compromise of integrity.

Making your work visible, documenting your contributions, building relationships with people who can advocate for you, writing and speaking about what you know — these are skills that help the people around you understand what you're contributing. They are not the same as performing without substance. They are the complement to substance that ensures substance is seen.

The person who does excellent work in silence and waits for it to be noticed is not taking a morally superior position. They are making a practical error in a world where visibility and capability are determined by different mechanisms. Developing visibility skills does not mean inflating your contribution — it means ensuring your real contribution is understood by the people who make decisions about opportunity.

>> The most durable path to recognition is combining genuine capability with the ability to make that capability visible to the people who make decisions about opportunity. Neither alone is sufficient. Together, they compound.

The most important tactical insight: visibility is built in accumulated small acts, not in single dramatic moments. The write-up after the project. The concise summary sent to the right person. The relationship maintained in the absence of immediate need. The speaking slot at the industry event. These are the inputs. The recognition that arrives later is the compounded output.
