A few years ago, I was talking to a plant manager at a manufacturing facility in Pune. He had recently taken on a batch of workers from a skill development centre in Odisha. I asked how they were performing.

He paused, then said something I didn't expect: "We prefer them."

Not "they're adequate." Not "they're fine." Prefer.

I have spent most of my career thinking about talent — where it concentrates, where it goes unnoticed, and what systems determine whose capability gets recognised. That conversation in Pune lodged in my mind because it ran against everything I had absorbed growing up in Odisha.

## The stigma I grew up with

In Odisha — and across most of India — skill-based work carried a specific hierarchy of shame. You became a technician, a fitter, an electrician, a welder, because other paths had closed. It was what happened when the engineering entrance exam didn't work out. The fallback, not the aspiration.

This was not unique to Odisha. India has spent a generation telling its children that the only worthy careers sat behind a desk: doctor, engineer, civil servant, software professional. Trade skills were something you did when you ran out of options.

The world, however, has been telling a different story.

## What the demand data actually shows

Germany has a structural shortage of skilled tradespeople. Australia is importing electricians. The Gulf construction economy runs on South Asian labour. Across developed markets, the scarcest workers are not generalist graduates — they are qualified technicians, trained operators, and certified tradespeople who can do physical work at international standards.

The demand is global. The question is who meets it.

A plumber trained to European standards is not competing in Bhubaneswar. An electrician certified for international installations is not competing in Cuttack. Their market is the world — and the world is paying wages that bear no resemblance to what the local market offers.

This is the opportunity that Odisha, quietly and without much national fanfare, has begun to access.

## What changed

What the state figured out — partly through policy, partly through necessity — is that skill development is not primarily a training problem. It is a dignity problem.

Nobody pursues a career they are ashamed of. Nobody invests years developing competence in a profession their family considers a last resort. The training infrastructure means nothing if the aspiration is absent.

The shift that happened in Odisha was not just the construction of better training facilities, though that mattered. It was the deliberate effort to make skilled trades feel different: modern campuses, professional uniforms, industry placements, and — critically — visible proof that this path leads somewhere.

When a young woman from a district town walks into a world-class welding facility and is told her skills will be in demand in Singapore, something changes. Not in the training room. In her understanding of what is possible for someone like her.

That is what development actually looks like from the inside.

## The premium is real

The "Odisha skill premium" — the perception among some employers that workers trained in Odisha's skill ecosystem carry stronger technical discipline and higher workplace readiness — is not a marketing claim. It is an observation I have heard from people who hire.

It exists because of something specific: when you build a training culture in a place where the alternative is scarce opportunity, you get a different quality of motivation. Workers from Odisha's industrial training system are not there because they had better options. They are there because they understood that this was the option — and they treated it accordingly.

Motivation that comes from genuine scarcity is different from motivation that comes from abundance. Employers notice it.

## What AI changes — and what it doesn't

The anxiety about artificial intelligence and employment is real but frequently aimed at the wrong target.

The categories of work that AI is genuinely disrupting — content generation, data processing, coding assistance, customer support at scale — are not the categories that build, install, repair, and maintain the physical world. The electrician wiring the data centre that runs the AI. The plumber maintaining the hospital. The construction technician building the infrastructure that every software company depends on. These jobs require physical judgment, accountability, and presence that cannot be offloaded to a model.

That does not mean skilled work is permanent or immune to change. It means that the disruption timeline is different — and that societies which treat skilled trades as a worthy investment now will be better positioned for whatever comes next.

## The difference between minerals and people

Odisha's minerals leave the state and generate wealth elsewhere. Iron ore goes to steel plants in other states. Bauxite becomes aluminium that is sold globally, with the value captured at the refinery, not the source. This is the standard resource trap: the raw material exports, the processing stays elsewhere, the tax base stays thin.

Skills are structurally different. Skills travel with the person.

A worker who leaves Odisha carrying world-class technical training generates remittances, builds relationships, and carries a reputation that accumulates — for themselves and for the state they came from. The next employer who hires from Odisha does so partly because of what the last one found. That compounds.

Odisha's greatest export may not be minerals, steel, or aluminium.

It may be people — not people leaving because opportunities are absent, but people carrying skills that are recognised and valued wherever they go.
