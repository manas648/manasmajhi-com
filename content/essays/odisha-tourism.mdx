When I bring visitors to Odisha - and I have brought a few, colleagues from Delhi, clients from Singapore, friends who had heard me talk about it for long enough that they finally wanted to see it - the reaction is consistent. They stand in front of the Konark Sun Temple and go quiet. They watch the flamingos settle on Chilika at dusk and say something like "I didn't know this was here." They walk through a tribal haat in Koraput and ask why nobody told them about this.

The gap between what Odisha has and what the world knows it has is the central fact of Odisha tourism. It is not a heritage problem. It is a visibility problem. And it is one of the more fixable gaps I see in this state.

Odisha has temple architecture that belongs in the same conversation as Angkor Wat. It has one of India's most culturally distinct tribal traditions, spread across the hill districts of the south and west. It has Chilika Lake, Asia's largest coastal lagoon. It has Bhitarkanika, one of India's finest mangrove ecosystems and a saltwater crocodile sanctuary. It has a coastline with beaches largely unknown to the national tourist market.

It receives a fraction of the tourism these assets should generate. Odisha attracts roughly 25–30 million domestic tourists annually - a significant number in absolute terms, but a fraction of what Rajasthan receives with comparable cultural assets, and far below what Kerala has built from a single natural feature (its backwaters) supported by accommodation investment and marketing focus. Foreign tourist arrivals, the segment most sensitive to international brand awareness and accommodation quality, remain below 300,000 annually. The gap is not explained by what's here. It is explained by what has not been built around it.

>> Odisha has temple architecture that belongs in the same conversation as Angkor Wat, and receives a fraction of its visitors. This is not a heritage problem. It is a visibility problem - and visibility problems are solvable.

## What Odisha has

**The Golden Triangle.** The combination of Bhubaneswar (the city of temples, historically over a thousand, with dozens of significant ones surviving), Puri (the Jagannath Temple and its beach), and Konark (the Sun Temple, a UNESCO World Heritage Site) forms one of the most historically and architecturally significant circuits in India. The Jagannath Rath Yatra, one of the world's largest religious processions, draws millions of visitors annually and has no direct parallel anywhere in India for scale and spectacle. The temples of Bhubaneswar - particularly the Lingaraj, Rajarani, and Mukteshwar - represent the Kalinga school of temple architecture: a distinctive tradition that predates the temples most Indian tourists associate with the country's heritage.

Most visitors who reach Konark leave saying the same thing: it should be as famous as Hampi. It isn't, yet. The infrastructure around it - the approach roads, the signage, the quality of guided interpretation, the accommodation within easy distance - has not yet matched the asset. That is changeable.

**Tribal culture and craft.** The southern and western districts of Odisha - Koraput, Rayagada, Kandhamal, Kalahandi - have among the most intact tribal traditions in India. The weekly tribal markets (haats), where Bonda, Kondh, Gadaba, and Kutia Kondh communities trade in goods and craft, are experiences that cultural tourists pay significant sums to access in far less authentic settings elsewhere in Asia. The textiles alone - Dongria Kondh weaving, Sambalpuri ikat, Odisha's Pattachitra paintings - are recognized globally by textile collectors and museum curators who have actually found them.

I grew up in Kalahandi. The weekly haats of that region were part of ordinary life when I was a child. I understand now, having traveled more widely, that what felt ordinary was actually rare. The preservation of that cultural texture, the variety, the specificity, the living practice - is not something you can replicate. It exists or it doesn't. In Odisha, it still does.

**Natural assets.** Chilika Lake, on the southern coast, is Asia's largest brackish water lagoon. It hosts one of India's largest populations of migratory birds in winter - flamingos, grey pelicans, herons, pintails - and has the Irrawaddy dolphin as a resident species. The dawn boat ride on Chilika in November or December, when the winter migrants are present, is one of the genuinely extraordinary wildlife experiences in India. Bhitarkanika, on the northern coast, is one of the few remaining large mangrove ecosystems in South Asia and has a significant saltwater crocodile population; its annual crocodile census has gained international media coverage. Simlipal National Park in Mayurbhanj is a tiger reserve and biosphere reserve with sal forests, waterfalls, and tribal villages.

**Coastline.** Odisha's 480-kilometre coast includes beaches that are largely underdeveloped compared to Goa or Kerala but have genuine quality. Puri's beach has a long domestic tourism history. Konark, Chandrabhaga, Chilika's mouth at Satapada, and Gopalpur beach in the south offer variety and significantly less crowding than their equivalents on the west coast - which, for a certain type of traveler, is the point.

## Why it underperforms

**Connectivity** is the primary structural constraint. Reaching Odisha from major Indian cities has historically required more connections, longer journey times, or higher costs than comparable destinations. Bhubaneswar Airport has improved significantly and now has direct services to multiple metros. But the secondary destinations - Jeypore for tribal tourism, Berhampur for the south coast, Sambalpur for western Odisha - remain poorly connected. A traveler who wants to visit the tribal haats of Koraput faces a journey that most people will not make unless they know exactly what awaits them.

**Accommodation quality** outside Bhubaneswar and Puri is limited. The budget and mid-market hotel infrastructure that allows leisure tourists to build multi-day itineraries exists primarily in these two cities. Beyond them, options thin quickly. This constrains the kind of itinerary that would significantly increase tourist spending per visit - and tourist spending per visit is the measure that makes tourism economically meaningful for local communities.

**Awareness and brand.** Odisha's tourism marketing has not historically competed with Rajasthan, Kerala, or Goa for the mental real estate of Indian or international tourists. Rajasthan owns "palace tourism." Kerala owns "backwaters and wellness." Goa owns "beach and nightlife." Odisha has not yet locked in the association that makes it come to mind when someone is planning. "Explore the Unexplored" is a start. It is not a finish.

**Last-mile access.** Tribal tourism, wildlife tourism, and coastal tourism all require reasonable roads to get from the nearest town to the destination. Some of the most extraordinary places in Odisha - the Niyamgiri hills, the Bonda highlands, remote sections of the Bhitarkanika coast - are difficult to reach and have limited facilities on arrival. The gap between the quality of the experience and the quality of the infrastructure to reach it is where Odisha loses visitors who would otherwise stay longer and spend more.

## What is changing

The trajectory has genuinely shifted in the last five years.

The Puri Heritage Corridor - a large-scale urban development connecting the Jagannath Temple to the sea through a redesigned heritage zone - is the most significant tourism infrastructure project Odisha has undertaken. It is not yet complete, but its scope is substantial and the improvement to the Puri experience, once finished, will be significant.

Chilika Development Authority's conservation work has visibly improved ecological quality at the lake. The dolphin and bird populations are more accessible from the improved jetties at Satapada. The Konark Dance Festival has grown nationally. The "Odisha, Explore the Unexplored" brand is more visible in national media than any previous state tourism positioning.

International tourism - primarily from neighbouring Asian countries, from Japan and Germany (both strong markets for wildlife and cultural tourism), and from the diaspora - is beginning to be developed. These are the visitors with the highest per-night spending and the most interest in what makes Odisha different rather than what it has in common with other Indian destinations.

>> The trajectory is upward. The scale of investment needed to match the asset base has not yet arrived - but the direction is clear, and the assets are not going anywhere.

## The economic case

Tourism is one of the few industries that creates employment broadly distributed across geographies - not concentrated in factories or offices, but in villages near wildlife sanctuaries, in coastal fishing communities near beaches, in the households of artisans whose craft becomes a souvenir for visitors. The multiplier effect of tourism spending is higher than most capital-intensive industries because it flows to accommodation, food, transport, and craft rather than into capital and raw materials.

This matters particularly for Odisha's rural and coastal districts, which the mineral and industrial economy - concentrated in the northern belt from Rourkela to Angul - reaches less directly. In Koraput, in Mayurbhanj, in the coastal districts south of Puri, tourism is one of the most available paths to income improvement that does not require industrial-scale capital investment or resettlement.

The assets are already there. They have been there for centuries. The work is access, quality, and awareness - a different and in some ways easier problem to solve than building an industrial base from scratch. Kerala did not create its backwaters. It built the experience around them. Rajasthan did not build new palaces. It made the existing ones accessible and marketed them aggressively. Odisha has work of comparable quality. The infrastructure and marketing around it is the task.
