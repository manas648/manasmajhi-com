When a VP hire fails at the 12-month mark, the most common post-mortem conclusion is: wrong person. Bad culture fit. Skills didn't translate.

These conclusions are almost always wrong — not factually, but diagnostically. The wrong person was hired because the search that produced them was running on bad intelligence. The market was misread. The candidate was assessed on the wrong signals. The decision was made by the wrong people with the wrong calibration.

Wrong person is a symptom. Bad intelligence is the disease.

The Intelligence Hiring System maps the three layers of intelligence that every executive search requires — and identifies where each layer most commonly fails.

## The architecture

```
LAYER 1: MARKET INTELLIGENCE
  ↓
  [Who exists? Where are they?
   What do they earn? What moves them?]

LAYER 2: TALENT INTELLIGENCE
  ↓
  [Who is exceptional vs. merely available?
   How do you distinguish signal from noise?]

LAYER 3: DECISION INTELLIGENCE
  [How do you evaluate fit, risk, and potential?
   How do you remove bias from the room?]
```

Each layer depends on the one above it. Talent intelligence without market intelligence means you're evaluating people you happened to find, not people from the relevant pool. Decision intelligence without talent intelligence means you're choosing well from a poorly assembled shortlist. The sequence is not optional.

## Layer 1: Market Intelligence

Market intelligence answers four questions before outreach begins.

**Who exists?** Not who is available — who exists. The best candidates for most VP and C-suite roles are not actively looking. They are employed, well-compensated, and being approached regularly. A search that only reaches actively looking candidates is searching a self-selected pool that skews toward people who have reasons to move — which is not the same as people who are exceptional.

**Where are they?** Geography, industry adjacency, function, and career stage. A VP of Engineering search for a 150-person SaaS company requires a different map than the same title at a 2,000-person enterprise. The candidates who look similar on paper are operating in different contexts, with different capabilities and different motivations. Market intelligence maps those distinctions before sourcing begins.

**What do they earn?** Compensation benchmarking is not a spreadsheet exercise — it is a real constraint that determines which candidates can realistically convert an offer. A search that produces a shortlist the company cannot afford to close is a failed search, regardless of candidate quality. Market intelligence includes compensation reality, not compensation aspiration.

**What moves them?** The candidates worth having are evaluating multiple things when they consider a role: the scope of the problem, the quality of the leadership team, the equity structure, the stage of the company, the cultural fit, and the risk profile. Outreach that doesn't address what moves this specific candidate produces low response rates and late-stage drop-off.

**Where this layer fails:** When the search starts with sourcing rather than mapping. When the brief is accepted as written without pressure-testing it against market reality. When compensation is not discussed until the offer stage. When the candidate pool is defined by the hiring manager's familiarity rather than by a genuine market map.

**Application pointer:** Before writing the first message to a candidate, answer all four questions in writing. If any of them requires significant uncertainty or estimation, the search is not ready to begin.

## Layer 2: Talent Intelligence

Talent intelligence answers the question the market map cannot: within the pool that exists, who is actually exceptional?

The volume problem in executive search is that the market contains many people who look qualified and far fewer who are genuinely right for a specific role at a specific company at a specific moment. Talent intelligence is the filter that distinguishes between them.

**Signal vs. noise.** The most common failure at this layer is treating noise as signal. Prestigious employer — signal or noise? It depends on what the person did there, not where they were. Impressive title — signal or noise? It depends on the scope and the context. Long tenure — signal or noise? It depends on whether the environment was growing or stagnant.

Talent intelligence requires building the capability to evaluate what a career actually demonstrates, not what it looks like. This is harder and slower than resume screening, and it is the only version that produces accurate shortlists.

**The brief as intelligence document.** A well-constructed candidate brief does not describe what the candidate has done. It demonstrates what they are capable of, under what conditions, with what evidence. The discipline of writing a brief that meets this standard forces the recruiter to have done the talent intelligence work — you cannot write that brief from a LinkedIn profile.

**Evidence of exceptional vs. evidence of adequate.** The exceptional candidate has done something specific that the adequate candidate hasn't. They built the team from 12 to 80 in 18 months. They closed the deal no one else could close. They turned around the product that was six months behind. Talent intelligence identifies those specifics and uses them as the filter — not because stories are always accurate, but because the ability to give specific evidence of exceptional outcomes is itself a signal.

**Where this layer fails:** When the shortlist is built from the most responsive candidates rather than the most capable ones. When the brief describes the role rather than evaluating the person. When the recruiter is assessing availability rather than fit. When the evaluation criteria are implicit rather than explicit — leaving the door open for bias to operate as judgement.

**Application pointer:** For each candidate on a shortlist, write one sentence that describes what they have done that someone adequate in this role has not. If you cannot write that sentence, they should not be on the list.

## Layer 3: Decision Intelligence

Decision intelligence is where most organisations are weakest, because it operates inside the company — not in the market — and the failures are invisible until they produce a bad hire.

**Who is in the room?** The single largest variable in hiring decision quality is who makes the decision and in what structure. A hiring committee dominated by one strong opinion will consistently select for candidates who match that opinion. A committee with no structured evaluation framework will select for the candidate they liked most, which is a proxy for familiarity and communication style, not capability.

**Calibration.** Decision intelligence requires that the people making the decision have calibrated their criteria against the actual requirements of the role — not against their preferred candidate type. Calibration is the discipline of testing: if I assess this specific competency, does my rating of this candidate predict their performance in this role? Most hiring teams have never done this exercise.

**Bias management.** Every hiring decision contains bias. The question is whether the bias is operating invisibly or being actively managed. The specific biases that most affect executive hiring: affinity bias (selecting for people who are similar), halo effect (one impressive credential inflating the overall assessment), and recency bias (the most recent interview performance dominating the evaluation). Decision intelligence means having explicit protocols that surface and counteract these.

**The reference call.** The most underused intelligence tool in executive hiring is the structured reference call. Not the courtesy confirmation call — the genuine investigative conversation with people who have managed, worked alongside, or been managed by the candidate in conditions similar to what they'll face in the new role. The information available from a well-conducted reference call is not available from any interview.

**Where this layer fails:** When the evaluation is informal. When criteria are not defined before interviews begin. When one person's strong preference dominates a committee without challenge. When references are collected but not genuinely interrogated. When the final decision is made on fit — a proxy for familiarity — rather than on evaluated evidence of capability.

**Application pointer:** Before the first interview, write down the three most important things the new VP must be able to do in the first 90 days. Use those three things as the explicit evaluation framework for every interview and reference call. Make the decision against that framework, not against general impressions.

## The failure mode of operating one layer at a time

The most common version of this failure: a company starts with a strong intuition about who they want (decision-layer thinking before market or talent intelligence), builds a process around finding that person, and then discovers — at offer stage or post-hire — that the intuition was built on incomplete information.

The layers exist in sequence for a reason. Market intelligence defines the realistic pool. Talent intelligence identifies the right candidates within it. Decision intelligence selects the right candidate from the shortlist with the minimum bias and the maximum rigour.

Skip a layer and the whole system produces garbage with confidence.

The confidence is the problem. Bad intelligence feels like judgement. Judgement that comes from incomplete market understanding, from a shortlist that was assembled rather than built, from a decision process that was intuitive rather than calibrated — that judgement produces the wrong hire, and the post-mortem calls it culture fit.

It isn't. It's a layer that wasn't built.
