I came across a stack of old letters recently and couldn't throw them away.

When I was studying in Delhi, far from Kalahandi, letters were how I stayed connected to the people I loved. Phone calls were expensive. A letter was not.

I would write home and wait.

Days would pass. Then one afternoon an envelope would arrive, carrying familiar handwriting across hundreds of kilometres. Before I even opened it, I already knew who it was from.

Those letters carried more than information. They carried presence.

The slight tilt of a sentence. The pressure of the pen. A line squeezed into the margin because someone had one last thing to say. You could feel the person behind them before you read a single word.

I have moved cities, changed phones, lost hard drives, forgotten passwords.

Those letters remain.

---

Handwriting possesses something technology struggles to replicate.

A typed sentence says: *I had something to tell you.*

A handwritten sentence says: *I took the time to sit down and tell you.*

>> A typed sentence says: I had something to tell you. A handwritten sentence says: I took the time to sit down and tell you.

You can see hesitation in a crossed-out word. You can sense excitement in hurried strokes. You can recognize the writer before reading their name. The page becomes more than a message — it becomes a trace of a life.

Perhaps that is why old letters are so difficult to throw away. The paper yellows, the ink fades, the folds soften — yet somehow their value increases. They stop being correspondence and become artifacts of affection.

---

I have always been drawn to things that carry evidence of time. An old fountain pen. A brass pocket watch. A weathered book. Pieces of furniture worn smooth by decades of use. Objects that were used, not displayed.

They bear the marks of what came before without apology.

In a world obsessed with the latest upgrade, there is something quietly dignified about a thing that has endured. Not because it is old, but because it was made to last — and did.

---

The world has become faster, smarter, and infinitely more convenient. Much of that progress is worth celebrating.

But as we gain convenience, some rituals quietly disappear.

The question is not old versus new. It is which human experiences are worth carrying forward, and which we are discarding thoughtlessly in the rush toward convenience.

The deliberate act of writing a letter — the feel of paper, the weight of a pen, thoughts moving at the pace of the hand — is inefficient by every modern measure. That may be exactly what makes it valuable.

And maybe that is still the rarest gift — not the one that arrives instantly, but the one that required someone to sit down, slow down, and mean it.
